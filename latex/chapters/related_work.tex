\section{Flattening Function Arguments}

The \texttt{tuple}-\texttt{PreFlatten} transformation introduced in this work
builds directly on the original \texttt{Flatten} transformation of
\citet{flat-paper}, but extends it to handle additional cases where the callers cannot be
``unified'' to a single flat/non-flat representation and pursues a simpler,
strictly-local analysis. See
\cref{sec:preflatten_comparison_flatten} for a detailed discussion.

\citet{mlton-closure-paper} introduced the MLton defunctionalization pass,
whose \texttt{datatype} objects we seek to eliminate through
our \texttt{ConApp}-\texttt{PreFlatten} transformation. The choice to
defunctionalize and lower to SSA-form IR in the middle-end is one of the
defining features of the MLton family of SML compilers -- implementations of the
SML-NJ lineage, by contrast, preserve higher-order functions and lower to a
continuation-passing style (CPS) middle-end IR. A risk of the MLton strategy is
that converting function calls to dispatch tables may introduce overhead in the
caller: our contribution is to eliminate this overhead in cases where the callee
is known statically.

\citet{higher-order-flattening} introduce a theoretical framework to solve the
problem of the \texttt{tuple}-\texttt{PreFlatten} transformation in the context
of a higher-order language, i.e. solving the SML-NJ-lineage (CPS IR) equivalent
of MLton's \citet{flat-paper}. We avoid the complexity that
\citet{higher-order-flattening} address by implementing our optimizations in
MLton's first-order \texttt{SSA} IR.

\citet{manticore-arity-paper} solve the problem of tuple flattening for
a CPS IR (as addressed by \citet{higher-order-flattening}) in the context of a
parallel dialect of SML. We, again, differ by targeting a first-order SSA IR.
We also differ in that their flattening heuristic is maximally conservative
(i.e. it never introduces unnecessary pack/unpack operations), whereas we
explore tradeoffs that favor more-aggressive flattening.

Recent work by \citet{closure-sharing-paper} examines the opposite optimization
strategy to our \texttt{ConApp}-\texttt{PreFlatten} optimization -- introducing
new layers of indirection in closure representations as a performance
optimization. Like \citet{manticore-arity-paper}, their analysis differs
substantially from anything in MLton, working in the context of an SML-NJ-style
(CPS-middle-end) compiler.

\citet{compiling-with-types} addresses the problem of tuple flattening
for SML in the context of separate compilation, where the concrete type of a
(source-language) polymorphic function may be unknown at compile time. Their
solution is to unconditionally interpret a tuple argument of member count $n <
k$ (for some limit $k$ selected for profitability) as a function call with $n$
values in registers, emitting code that inspects the argument type at runtime in
order to handle the case where the concrete type is unknown.
Our \texttt{tuple}-\texttt{PreFlatten} transformation differs by targeting a
monomorphic IR, and by employing a more limited flattening heuristic.

\citet{haskell-fc-unbox} introduce a strategy for tuple flattening in the
context of an IR that is both higher-order and non-strict. Their solution is to
rewrite a function with tuple arguments into a ``wrapper/worker'' pair, where
the ``wrapper'' is an unpack sequence followed by a call to the ``worker'', and
the ``worker'' is a re-pack sequence followed by the original function body.
Their rewrite is conditional on a strictness analyis, which must prove that
evaluating the tuple's producing thunk does not cause the program to diverge.
The net effect of inlining the ``wrapper'' at a callsite preceded by a pack
operation is identical to our \texttt{tuple}-\texttt{PreFlatten}; our approach
differs in that we \textit{only} flatten at callsites containing pack
operations, avoiding overhead for unflattenable callers (at the potential cost
of code size).

\citet{haskell-callspec} presents a strategy for emitting specializations of
functions when certain ``call patterns'' appear in the source program. In
essence, a ``call pattern'' is a per-argument pattern-match for callsites where
the tag of a \texttt{datatype} object is known statically; it is therefore
closely related to the \texttt{ConApp}-\texttt{PreFlatten} transformation presented
here. Since \citet{haskell-callspec} targets GHC, like \citet{haskell-fc-unbox},
its optimizations are implemented for a lazy, higher-order polymorphic language
-- consequently, they choose different cost heuristics (e.g. only specializing
recursive functions), and are unable to make specialization decisions on the
basis of function-valued arguments (which we naturally subsume under
\texttt{datatype} constructor matching in the context of MLton's first-order
IR).

\citet{local-paper} and \citet{adt4hpc-paper} aim to achieve a similar goal to
our \texttt{ConApp} flattening (eliminating indirections while traversing ADTs),
but address it in a different context: they are concerned with manual control
over the format of serialized data on disk, rather than (like us) specializing
the in-memory format of data based on its users (while, jointly, specializing
users according to the in-memory format).

\citet{double-steal-paper} also investigates a similar problem to our
\texttt{ConApp} flattening (eliminating pointers introduced by ADTs in the
source language); however their contribution focuses on a novel, lossless
in-memory representation for the ADT rather than optimizing out the ADT metadata
entirely.

\citet{bbv-paper} apply similar techniques to our \texttt{ConApp} flattening
(i.e. emitting specialized code based on callers) in the context of optimizing a
dynamically-typed language. Our work differs from theirs in that we specialize
only on properties that we can guarantee from the type system, and so both our
policy decisions and our implementation mechanism are greatly simplified (the
latter relying entirely on standard, pre-existing optimization techniques to
``clean up'' in the specialized blocks).

The equivalent transformation in C/C++ compilers like \texttt{clang} or
\texttt{gcc} is referred to as ``scalar replacement of aggregates,'' (SROA) as
described by \citet{munchick-compiler-design}. Since these languages natively
support ``unboxed'' \texttt{struct} value types, the goal in this context is to
enable subsequent optimizations like constant or copy propagation, rather than
to eliminate heap allocations. In order for the transformation to be sound, the
compiler must prove that no references to the aggregate escape the current
function, since (like our tuple flattening), the transformation cannot preserve
\hl{pointers to the original object}. Due to separate compilation and fixed ABI
constraints, \texttt{clang} and \texttt{gcc} do not, in general, have the
freedom to change function signatures in the style of our \texttt{PreFlatten}.

\section{\texttt{array}, \texttt{vector} Flattening}

\citet{dataonly-paper} apply a similar transformation (tuple flattening) in a
similar context: in fact, their ``data-only flattening'' is roughly the SoA
transformation that we apply. Our SoA transformation differs from theirs by
lacking shape descriptors. We differ from their approach by a) considering a
broader class of flattening strategies, and b) applying our pass in tandem with
the existing flattening passes to eliminate intermediate tuples, rather than
relying on a single huge pass to do the fusion; i.e. we have a simpler
transformation by operating at a higher level of abstraction in the compiler and
leveraging pre-existing optimizations.

Various work at Jane Street for unboxed types in OCaml aims to materialize the
same wins that we demonstrate here through the SoA transformation, as discussed
in e.g. \citet{layout-poly-paper}. However, the primary technical challenge that
they seek to overcome -- selecting the appropriate function specialization for a
potentially-unboxed type -- does not apply to MPL: since MLton does whole-program
compilation, we always know at compile time which specializations are necessary
to emit.

\citet{futhark-flatten} also discuss the problem of choosing an optimum
flattened representation from among the possible choices -- here on the GPU for
the Futhark programming language. Their problem differs from ours in that MPL
resolves the issue of work imbalance between functional units (CPU cores or GPU
SMs) through low-overhead scheduling of tasks at runtime, rather than at compile
time, as explored by \citet{mpl-sched-paper}.

\citet{realtime-sml-paper} also investigate the impact of deep flattening
optimizations on performance: the authors there disable \texttt{DeepFlatten} in
order to avoid complexity when computing byte offsets for
post-\texttt{DeepFlatten} arrays whose flattened tuples contained elements of
different sizes. We avoid this issue by simply restricting ourselves to tuples
with uniform element types and observe that this is sufficient for all
opportunities in our benchmark suites.

\citet{virgil-paper} explores an even more-aggressive tradeoff than the
flattening optimizations discussed here, choosing to flatten-away all tuple data
structures in their compilation pipeline: for variables and arguments this is
equivalent to plain \texttt{Flatten}; for arrays they choose between SoA and AoS
``[d]epending on the compilation target.'' In line with our observations above,
they remark that extreme flattening can be harmful for large tuples.

\citet{automating-adhoc-paper} propose a generic mechanism (in the context of
the Scala compiler) to allow programmers to regions of code with representation
decisions (e.g. all arays within a particular region use an SoA representation,
or all 32-bit complex numbers use a packed 64-bit representation). Their
approach is similar to ours in that their transformation inserts ``coercion''
operations at the boundary of such regions in order to ensure the correct
representations (analogously to our inserted pack/unpack sequences); their
approach is different in that both the transformation heuristic and the data
representations are provided by the programmer.

\citet{object-inlining} address the problem of the MLton \texttt{DeepFlatten}
pass in the context of object oriented languages, proposing a mechanism to
``flatten'' nested objects by inlining pointed-to children. \citet{java-scicomp}
extend the mechanism of \citet{object-inlining} in order to replace
array-of-pointers containers in Java with the corresponding struct-of-arrays
representation, like our \texttt{SoA}-\texttt{ShallowFlatten}. Their approach
differs from ours in that (like \texttt{DeepFlatten}) they restrict their
optimization to cases where no coercion pack/unpack sequences are required.

AoS container layouts have a long history in the literature of data-parallel
computing. In the context of functional, SML-like languages, it was first
introduced by way of the NESL programming language \citep{nesl-book} (which
also proposed the \texttt{quickhull} and \texttt{linearrec} benchmarks that
appear in this work), in which AoS is the canonical layout for
container-of-tuple types. The Data Parallel Haskell project of
\citet{data-parallel-haskell}, likewise, commits exclusively to AoS layouts for
in-memory representations. Our approach differs from theirs by operating in the
context of a language whose default representation is array-of-pointers.

In the broader computer architecture context, a major motivation for flat
AoS/SoA layouts is to enable SIMD parallelism -- in fact, the ``AoS/SoA''
terminology itself was coined in the work of \citet{intel-simd-journal}, which introduced
the SSE instruction set extension for the Pentium III processor. While this work
does not leverage SIMD parallelism, we hope that the \texttt{ShallowFlatten}
transformation can enable future investigation in this area for both MPL and
MLton.
